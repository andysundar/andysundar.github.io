# Variational Autoencoder (VAE) — Deep Step-by-Step Notes

## 1. Core Idea

A **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)** is a probabilistic version of an autoencoder.

A basic autoencoder learns:

$$
x \rightarrow z \rightarrow \hat{x}
$$

where:

* $x$ = input
* $z$ = latent representation
* $\hat{x}$ = reconstruction

A VAE changes one very important thing:

Instead of mapping an input to **one fixed latent vector** $z$, it maps the input to a **probability distribution** over latent vectors.

So instead of saying:

> “This input becomes exactly this latent code”

it says:

> “This input is represented by a latent Gaussian distribution, and I can sample from it.”

That means the encoder outputs:

* a mean vector: $\mu$
* a spread / uncertainty vector: usually standard deviation $\sigma$ or log variance $\log \sigma^2$

Then we sample:

$$
z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

and decode that $z$ to reconstruct the input.

---

# 2. Why This Matters

A basic autoencoder is good at compression, but it does not guarantee that the latent space is smooth, continuous, or good for generation.

A VAE solves that.

It learns a latent space where:

* nearby latent points generate similar outputs
* latent space is organized
* new samples can be generated by sampling from a known distribution

This makes VAEs useful for:

* generative modeling
* learning smooth latent spaces
* interpolation between samples
* anomaly detection
* representation learning

---

# 3. Intuition First

Imagine you want to store handwritten digits in a compressed space.

A normal autoencoder might map one digit “3” to some arbitrary code.

But another “3” might go to a completely unrelated place in latent space.

That makes generation difficult.

A VAE forces the latent space to behave more like a structured cloud, often close to a standard normal distribution:

$$
z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

That means:

* similar inputs land in nearby regions
* the space between known examples is meaningful
* random sampling becomes possible

---

# 4. Main Difference from a Standard Autoencoder

## Standard Autoencoder

Encoder:

$$
z = f_{\text{enc}}(x)
$$

Decoder:

$$
\hat{x} = f_{\text{dec}}(z)
$$

Loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(x, \hat{x})
$$

---

## Variational Autoencoder

Encoder produces parameters of a distribution:

$$
\mu = f_\mu(x)
$$

$$
\log \sigma^2 = f_{\log \sigma^2}(x)
$$

Then sample:

$$
z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

Decoder:

$$
\hat{x} = f_{\text{dec}}(z)
$$

Loss has two parts:

1. **Reconstruction loss**
2. **KL divergence regularization**

Total loss:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}*{\text{recon}} + \mathcal{L}*{\text{KL}}
$$

---

# 5. The Big Picture Pipeline

For one input $x$:

1. Feed $x$ into encoder
2. Encoder outputs $\mu$ and $\log \sigma^2$
3. Convert to standard deviation $\sigma$
4. Sample noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$
5. Form latent vector:
   $$
   z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon
   $$
6. Feed $z$ into decoder
7. Produce reconstruction $\hat{x}$
8. Compute total loss

---

# 6. Why Not Sample Directly?

If we sample directly:

$$
z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

then gradient flow through sampling becomes difficult.

Training by backpropagation needs differentiable computation.

So VAEs use the **reparameterization trick**:

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

Now:

* $\epsilon$ is the random part
* $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are deterministic outputs of the encoder
* gradients can flow through $\mu$ and $\sigma$

This is one of the most important ideas in VAEs.

---

# 7. Mathematical Definition

Let input be:

$$
x \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

Let latent variable be:

$$
z \in \mathbb{R}^m
$$

Usually $m < n$.

The encoder defines an approximate posterior distribution:

$$
q_\phi(z|x)
$$

where:

* $q$ = approximate distribution
* $\phi$ = encoder parameters

In a standard VAE, this is modeled as a diagonal Gaussian:

$$
q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(z; \mu(x), \text{diag}(\sigma^2(x)))
$$

That means each latent dimension has:

* its own mean
* its own variance
* no covariance with other dimensions

The decoder defines a likelihood:

$$
p_\theta(x|z)
$$

where:

* $\theta$ = decoder parameters

And we assume a prior over latent variables:

$$
p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

---

# 8. What the VAE Is Actually Trying to Do

The real goal is to maximize the probability of observed data:

$$
p_\theta(x)
$$

But this is difficult because:

$$
p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x|z)p(z),dz
$$

This integral is usually intractable.

So instead of maximizing $\log p_\theta(x)$ directly, we maximize a lower bound called the **ELBO**:

$$
\log p_\theta(x) \ge \mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p(z))
$$

This is the core equation of the VAE.

---

# 9. Meaning of the ELBO Terms

## First term

$$
\mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]
$$

This says:

> If I sample latent codes from the encoder’s distribution, how well can the decoder reconstruct the input?

This is the reconstruction quality term.

---

## Second term

$$
D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p(z))
$$

This says:

> How far is the encoder’s latent distribution from the desired prior distribution?

This regularizes latent space so it stays close to standard normal.

---

## Intuition

The model is balancing two goals:

1. reconstruct the input well
2. keep latent distributions simple and well-organized

That tension is the heart of a VAE.

---

# 10. Loss Used in Practice

We usually **minimize** negative ELBO:

$$
\mathcal{L}*{\text{VAE}} = -\mathbb{E}*{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] + D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p(z))
$$

This becomes:

$$
\mathcal{L}*{\text{VAE}} = \mathcal{L}*{\text{recon}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}
$$

---

# 11. Reconstruction Loss in Practical Form

Depending on data type:

## For continuous data

Use mean squared error or Gaussian likelihood:

$$
\mathcal{L}*{\text{recon}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum*{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2
$$

## For binary or normalized pixel data

Use binary cross entropy:

$$
\mathcal{L}*{\text{recon}} =
-\sum*{i=1}^{n}\left[x_i\log \hat{x}_i + (1-x_i)\log(1-\hat{x}_i)\right]
$$

---

# 12. KL Divergence Term for Gaussian Latents

This is the most famous VAE formula.

If:

$$
q_\phi(z|x)=\mathcal{N}(\mu, \text{diag}(\sigma^2))
$$

and prior is:

$$
p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

then:

$$
D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p(z)) =
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2 - 1\right)
$$

Often we store:

$$
\log \sigma_j^2 = \text{logvar}_j
$$

Then:

$$
\sigma_j^2 = e^{\text{logvar}_j}
$$

So KL becomes:

$$
D_{KL} =
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\mu_j^2 + e^{\text{logvar}_j} - \text{logvar}_j - 1\right)
$$

This is the version most implementations use.

---

# 13. Why This KL Formula Makes Sense

It penalizes three situations:

1. **Mean too far from zero**

   * $\mu_j^2$ gets large

2. **Variance too large**

   * $\sigma_j^2$ gets large

3. **Variance too small**

   * $-\log \sigma_j^2$ becomes large when variance is tiny

So the KL term encourages each latent dimension to stay near:

$$
\mu_j \approx 0, \quad \sigma_j^2 \approx 1
$$

That makes the latent space resemble a standard normal distribution.

---

# 14. Full Forward Pass Step by Step

Suppose:

* input dimension = $n$
* hidden dimension = $h$
* latent dimension = $m$

Then:

## Encoder hidden layer

$$
h = \phi(W_1 x + b_1)
$$

## Mean head

$$
\mu = W_\mu h + b_\mu
$$

## Log-variance head

$$
\log \sigma^2 = W_{\logvar} h + b_{\logvar}
$$

## Convert log variance to standard deviation

$$
\sigma = \exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\log \sigma^2\right)
$$

## Sample noise

$$
\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

## Reparameterize

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon
$$

## Decode

$$
h_d = \phi(W_2 z + b_2)
$$

$$
\hat{x} = \psi(W_3 h_d + b_3)
$$

---

# 15. Small Numerical Example

Let latent dimension be 2.

Suppose for one input, encoder outputs:

$$
\mu =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5 \
-0.2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\log \sigma^2 =
\begin{bmatrix}
-0.7 \
0.0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then:

$$
\sigma^2 =
\begin{bmatrix}
e^{-0.7} \
e^0
\end{bmatrix}
=============

\begin{bmatrix}
0.4966 \
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

So standard deviation:

$$
\sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
\sqrt{0.4966} \
1
\end{bmatrix}
\approx
\begin{bmatrix}
0.7047 \
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now sample:

$$
\epsilon =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.3 \
-1.1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then:

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon
$$

$$
z =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5 \
-0.2
\end{bmatrix}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
0.7047 \cdot 0.3 \
1 \cdot (-1.1)
\end{bmatrix}
=============

\begin{bmatrix}
0.5 + 0.2114 \
-0.2 - 1.1
\end{bmatrix}
=============

\begin{bmatrix}
0.7114 \
-1.3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

That sampled latent vector is what the decoder sees.

---

# 16. Computing the KL Term Numerically

Using the same $\mu$ and $\log \sigma^2$:

$$
\mu_1 = 0.5,\quad \mu_2=-0.2
$$

$$
\text{logvar}_1=-0.7,\quad \text{logvar}_2=0
$$

Then:

$$
D_{KL}
======

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left(\mu_j^2 + e^{\text{logvar}_j} - \text{logvar}_j - 1\right)
$$

For $j=1$:

$$
0.5^2 + e^{-0.7} - (-0.7) - 1
= 0.25 + 0.4966 + 0.7 - 1
= 0.4466
$$

For $j=2$:

$$
(-0.2)^2 + e^0 - 0 - 1
= 0.04 + 1 - 1
= 0.04
$$

Total:

$$
D_{KL} = \frac{1}{2}(0.4466 + 0.04) = \frac{1}{2}(0.4866)=0.2433
$$

So latent regularization loss is:

$$
0.2433
$$

If reconstruction loss were, say, $0.58$, then total loss would be:

$$
0.58 + 0.2433 = 0.8233
$$

---

# 17. First-Principles View of Why This Works

A standard autoencoder only asks:

> Can you compress and reconstruct?

A VAE asks something stronger:

> Can you compress and reconstruct while making the latent representations come from a well-behaved probability distribution?

This matters because then we can do:

* sampling
* interpolation
* smooth generation

Without KL regularization, encoder outputs can become scattered and irregular.

With KL regularization, the latent space becomes smoother and more structured.

---

# 18. Geometric Intuition

Think of the latent space as a 2D plane.

A standard autoencoder may place data in disconnected weird islands.

A VAE encourages those islands to become a smoother cloud centered near zero.

So:

* sampling near the origin makes sense
* moving gradually in latent space changes output gradually
* interpolation between two examples becomes meaningful

---

# 19. Why Reconstruction Alone Is Not Enough

Suppose two training examples map to very distant arbitrary points, even if they are similar.

Then if you sample between them, decoder may output nonsense.

That is because the latent space has “holes”.

The KL term reduces this by encouraging every encoded sample to live in a common regularized latent structure.

---

# 20. Reparameterization Trick in More Detail

Sampling is written as:

$$
z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

But direct sampling hides the dependence on $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

So we rewrite:

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon
$$

where:

$$
\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

This separates:

* randomness = $\epsilon$
* learnable deterministic part = $\mu, \sigma$

Now gradients flow through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ during backprop.

Without this trick, ordinary gradient-based learning is hard.

---

# 21. Why Use Log Variance Instead of Variance Directly?

The encoder usually outputs:

$$
\text{logvar} = \log \sigma^2
$$

not $\sigma^2$ directly.

Reasons:

1. variance must always be positive
2. log variance can be any real number
3. it is numerically more stable

Then recover standard deviation using:

$$
\sigma = \exp(0.5 \cdot \text{logvar})
$$

---

# 22. Minimal Pseudocode

```text
for each input x:

    # encoder
    h = encoder_hidden(x)
    mu = mu_head(h)
    logvar = logvar_head(h)

    # reparameterization
    std = exp(0.5 * logvar)
    epsilon = random_normal_vector(latent_dim)
    z = mu + std * epsilon

    # decoder
    x_hat = decoder(z)

    # reconstruction loss
    recon_loss = loss(x, x_hat)

    # KL divergence
    kl = 0
    for j in range(latent_dim):
        kl += mu[j]*mu[j] + exp(logvar[j]) - logvar[j] - 1
    kl = 0.5 * kl

    total_loss = recon_loss + kl

    backpropagate(total_loss)
    update_parameters()
```

# 29. ELBO Derivation Intuition

We want:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
$$

Introduce $q_\phi(z|x)$ and write:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
================

\mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}
\left[
\log p_\theta(x)
\right]
$$

Then rewrite:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
================

\mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}
\left[
\log \frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{p_\theta(z|x)}
\right]
$$

Add and subtract $\log q_\phi(z|x)$:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
================

\mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}
\left[
\log \frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}
\right]
+
D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p_\theta(z|x))
$$

Since KL divergence is always nonnegative:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
\ge
\mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}
\left[
\log \frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}
\right]
$$

This lower bound becomes:

$$
\mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p(z))
$$

That is the ELBO.

---

# 30. Why the ELBO Is a Lower Bound

Because:

$$
D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p_\theta(z|x)) \ge 0
$$

So:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
================

\text{ELBO}
+
D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p_\theta(z|x))
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{ELBO} \le \log p_\theta(x)
$$

Maximizing ELBO means:

* improving data likelihood
* making approximate posterior closer to true posterior

---

# 31. Standard VAE Architecture

A common network layout is:

$$
x \rightarrow h \rightarrow (\mu, \log \sigma^2) \rightarrow z \rightarrow h_d \rightarrow \hat{x}
$$

So the encoder splits into two heads:

* one for mean
* one for log variance

This is different from a standard autoencoder, where encoder usually outputs only one vector.

---

# 32. Comparison with Standard Autoencoder

| Feature                  | Standard Autoencoder | Variational Autoencoder |
| ------------------------ | -------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Encoder output           | Fixed latent vector  | Distribution parameters |
| Latent representation    | Deterministic        | Probabilistic           |
| Sampling ability         | Poor                 | Strong                  |
| Regularized latent space | Not guaranteed       | Yes                     |
| Generative model         | Weak                 | Yes                     |
| Loss                     | Reconstruction only  | Reconstruction + KL     |

---

# 33. What Does the KL Term Really Do?

It prevents the encoder from using arbitrarily sharp and isolated encodings.

Without KL:

* each input may get its own narrow code
* decoder memorizes
* latent space becomes messy

With KL:

* encoded distributions stay near prior
* space is smoother
* model generalizes better in latent space

---

# 34. Beta-VAE

A very important variant is **Beta-VAE**.

It modifies the loss:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}*{\text{recon}} + \beta \mathcal{L}*{\text{KL}}
$$

where $\beta > 0$ controls the strength of latent regularization.

## If $\beta = 1$

Standard VAE.

## If $\beta > 1$

Stronger regularization:

* more disentangled latent features
* worse reconstruction sometimes

## If $\beta < 1$

Weaker regularization:

* better reconstruction
* less structured latent space

---

# 37. Common Problems

## 1. Posterior collapse

The decoder ignores latent variable $z$ and reconstructs using its own power.

Then:

$$
q_\phi(z|x) \approx p(z)
$$

and KL becomes tiny.

### Why bad?

Latent space stops carrying useful information.

### Common fixes

* KL annealing
* weaker decoder
* Beta-VAE tuning
* skip connections carefully
* free bits / minimum KL tricks

---

## 2. Blurry reconstructions

Especially common in image VAEs.

### Why?

Likelihood assumptions like Gaussian decoding often average over possibilities.

### Result

Generated images may look smooth or blurry.

---

## 3. KL too strong

If KL dominates:

* latent codes become too close to zero
* reconstructions become poor

### Fix

Tune KL weight or use annealing.

---

# 38. KL Annealing

A common training trick is:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}*{\text{recon}} + \lambda(t)\mathcal{L}*{\text{KL}}
$$

where $\lambda(t)$ starts small and grows during training.

This lets model first learn reconstruction, then later organize latent space.

Example:

* epoch 1: $\lambda = 0.01$
* epoch 20: $\lambda = 0.2$
* epoch 50: $\lambda = 1.0$

---

# 39. Anomaly Detection with VAE

The idea:

* train VAE on normal data
* normal data reconstructs well
* abnormal data often reconstructs poorly or lands in strange latent regions

Use:

* reconstruction loss
* KL value
* or both

Example:

* machine sensor failure detection
* unusual patient state detection
* fraudulent patterns

# 40. From-Scratch Language-Agnostic Pseudocode

```text
initialize encoder weights
initialize mu head weights
initialize logvar head weights
initialize decoder weights

for epoch in 1..num_epochs:
    total_loss = 0

    for each sample x:

        # ---- encoder ----
        h = encoder_forward(x)
        mu = linear_mu(h)
        logvar = linear_logvar(h)

        # ---- sample ----
        std = exp(0.5 * logvar)
        epsilon = sample_standard_normal(latent_dim)
        z = mu + std * epsilon

        # ---- decoder ----
        x_hat = decoder_forward(z)

        # ---- losses ----
        recon_loss = reconstruction_loss(x, x_hat)

        kl = 0
        for j in 0..latent_dim-1:
            kl += mu[j]*mu[j] + exp(logvar[j]) - logvar[j] - 1
        kl = 0.5 * kl

        loss = recon_loss + kl

        # ---- backward ----
        compute gradients of reconstruction part
        compute gradients of KL part wrt mu and logvar
        combine gradients
        update all weights with gradient descent

        total_loss += loss

    print(epoch, total_loss / num_samples)
```

---

# 41. How KL Contributes Gradients

Recall:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{KL} =
\frac{1}{2}\sum_j \left(\mu_j^2 + e^{\text{logvar}_j} - \text{logvar}_j - 1\right)
$$

Derivative with respect to mean:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{KL}}{\partial \mu_j} = \mu_j
$$

Derivative with respect to log variance:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{KL}}{\partial \text{logvar}_j}
=

\frac{1}{2}(e^{\text{logvar}_j} - 1)
$$

These are simple and very useful in manual implementation.

---

# 42. Decoder Output Choices

## For binary inputs or normalized pixels

Use sigmoid output:

$$
\hat{x} \in [0,1]
$$

## For continuous data

Use identity output.

The decoder output should match the data type and reconstruction loss.

# 44. Why VAEs Are So Important Historically

VAEs became a major milestone because they connected:

* deep learning
* probabilistic latent variable models
* variational inference
* generative modeling

They showed how to train latent-variable generative models efficiently with neural networks and backpropagation.

This was a major conceptual bridge between classical probabilistic modeling and modern deep learning.

# 45. Research Insight

VAEs are important not because they always produce the sharpest samples, but because they introduced a very deep idea:

> Learn a distribution over compressed representations, not just a point representation.

That idea influenced many later models:

* disentanglement research
* latent diffusion approaches
* probabilistic sequence models
* structured representation learning

---

# 46. Mental Model

A standard autoencoder says:

> “Give me the most compressed code.”

A VAE says:

> “Give me a compressed probabilistic region in latent space, and make those regions collectively form a nice global distribution.”

That is why a VAE is both:

* an encoder-decoder model
* a generative probabilistic model

---

# 47. Summary

A Variational Autoencoder is an encoder-decoder network that learns a **distribution** over latent codes instead of a single deterministic code. The encoder outputs mean and variance parameters of a Gaussian distribution, the latent vector is sampled using the reparameterization trick, and the decoder reconstructs the input from that sample. Training minimizes reconstruction error plus KL divergence to a prior, usually standard normal. This produces a smooth, structured latent space that supports sampling, interpolation, and generative modeling.

# 48. Quick Revision Sheet

## Core equations

Encoder:

$$
q_\phi(z|x)=\mathcal{N}(\mu(x), \text{diag}(\sigma^2(x)))
$$

Prior:

$$
p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

Sampling:

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon,\quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)
$$

ELBO:

$$
\mathbb{E}*{q*\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)|p(z))
$$

KL term:

$$
D_{KL} =
\frac{1}{2}\sum_j\left(\mu_j^2 + e^{\text{logvar}_j} - \text{logvar}_j - 1\right)
$$
